Matcha is one of the oldest and most fascinating drinks in the world. Here are
thirteen facts that explain why it looks, tastes, and feels the way it does.

## 1. Matcha and green tea come from the same plant

Matcha, green tea, black tea, and oolong all come from one plant:
*Camellia sinensis*. The difference is entirely in how the leaves are grown and
processed, not in the plant itself.

## 2. You drink the whole leaf, not an infusion

Most teas are steeped and the leaves are thrown away. Matcha is different: the
leaves are ground into a fine powder and whisked into water, so you consume the
entire leaf. That is why a bowl of matcha delivers far more of the leaf's
compounds than a steeped cup of green tea.

## 3. The plants are shade-grown before harvest

For roughly three to four weeks before picking, matcha plants are covered to
block most sunlight. The plant responds by producing more chlorophyll (its
vivid green color) and more L-theanine (the amino acid behind matcha's calm
focus). Shading is the single most important step in what makes matcha, matcha.

## 4. "Matcha" literally means "ground tea"

The word 抹茶 combines *matsu* (to rub or grind) and *cha* (tea). The name is a
plain description of how it is made.

## 5. It is stone-ground, very slowly

Traditional matcha is milled between two granite stones. The process is
deliberately slow, often producing only 30 to 40 grams of powder per hour,
because grinding too fast generates heat that would scorch the delicate leaf
and dull its flavor.

## 6. Color is a quality signal

Good matcha is a vibrant, almost electric green. A dull, yellowish, or brownish
tint usually means older or lower-grade leaves, or matcha that has oxidized with
age. When in doubt, trust the green.

## 7. Ceremonial and culinary are different grades

Ceremonial-grade matcha is made from the youngest first-harvest leaves and is
meant to be whisked with water and sipped on its own. Culinary-grade is made
from later, more mature leaves and is built to hold its flavor in lattes,
smoothies, and baking.

## 8. It has caffeine, but it behaves differently

Matcha contains caffeine, but it is paired with L-theanine, which smooths out
the release. The result is steady, sustained energy rather than the sharp peak
and crash of coffee.

## 9. It has been part of Japanese culture for centuries

Powdered tea was brought to Japan from China by Zen Buddhist monks in the late
1100s. It became central to the Japanese tea ceremony (*chanoyu*), a practice
built around mindfulness, hospitality, and presence.

## 10. First-harvest leaves are the most prized

The first harvest of the year, called *ichibancha*, produces the sweetest,
most nutrient-dense leaves. Avora is made entirely from first-harvest leaves.

## 11. Kyoto and Kagoshima are two of the great regions

Japan's most respected matcha comes from a handful of regions. Kyoto (home to
the historic Uji growing area) and Kagoshima in the south are two of the most
celebrated. Avora sources from both.

## 12. Whisking technique changes the taste

The same matcha can taste clumpy and bitter or smooth and frothy depending on
how you whisk it. Brisk "W" or "M" strokes for about 30 seconds aerate the tea
and break up clumps, which is why technique matters as much as the powder.

## 13. Freshness fades fast once opened

Matcha is a fresh product. Once a tin is opened and exposed to air, light, and
heat, its color and flavor start to fade. Store it sealed, cool, and dark, and
finish an opened tin within a few weeks.
